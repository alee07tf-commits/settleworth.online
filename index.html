What to do with the number

You will get a range in a few seconds. If any of these three describe your case, a more specific tool will give you a sharper number.

  1. Was a commercial truck or an 18-wheeler involved?

    Truck claims are argued against far larger policies, and often against more than one insurer — the carrier, the driver and sometimes the loader. The same injuries can be worth several times a car claim, so run the truck accident settlement calculator instead of the general tool.

  2. Was your car repaired rather than written off?

    Even after flawless repairs, a car with a crash on its history report sells for less, and that loss is a separate claim your injury range does not include. Put a number on it with the diminished value claim calculator.

  3. Are you left with numbness, tingling or weakness?

    Nerve injuries are valued on permanence rather than on bills, which is why they carry the highest multipliers in the table. If an EMG confirmed the damage, use the nerve damage settlement calculator for a range scaled to that.

How it works

A transparent method, not a black box

We use the same approaches attorneys and insurers use — and we show our work. No method is hidden behind a contact form.

01

Multiplier method

Pain & suffering = medical costs × a severity multiplier (1.5–5). Higher for serious, permanent injuries.

02

Per-diem method

A daily dollar value × the number of recovery days — an alternative way to value the same harm.

03

State adjustment

Your state's comparative-negligence rule and your share of fault are applied automatically to the result.

How much is my settlement worth?

The honest answer is that it is a range, and you can work out your own in two steps. First add the costs with a receipt behind them: medical bills to date, treatment still to come, pay you have lost, pay you will lose, and property damage. That total is your economic damages, and it is the only part of a claim that is not argued. Second, apply one of the two accepted methods to the part that has no receipts — a multiplier of roughly 1.5 to 5 on those economic damages, or a daily rate across your recovery. Then subtract your share of fault, under your state's rule.

Two inputs move the answer more than everything else combined. One is future medical cost, which most people leave blank and which is often the largest single line in a serious claim. The other is your share of fault, because it is the number an insurer is financially motivated to argue up, and in five jurisdictions any share of it at all can end the claim outright. A third sits outside the arithmetic entirely: a claim can never exceed the insurance coverage available, however serious the injury.
