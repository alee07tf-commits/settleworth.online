The method

How pain, suffering and fees are calculated

This category values specific pieces of a claim rather than a whole case. Pain and suffering — the non-economic harm — is estimated two recognised ways. The multiplier method takes your economic damages (medical bills plus lost wages) and multiplies them by roughly 1.5 to 5 depending on severity and permanence. The per-diem method instead assigns a daily dollar value for every day of recovery. The same injury can produce two different figures, which is why seeing both side by side is useful.

The other half of "what you actually keep" is fees. Most personal-injury attorneys work on contingency — typically 33–40% of the settlement — and case costs (filing, experts, records) often come out on top of that. So a headline settlement and your net recovery are very different numbers. A fee breakdown shows the gap before you sign anything, and reminds you that a settlement amount tied to a physical injury is frequently non-taxable while lost-wage and punitive portions may not be.

What drives value

What changes the pain-and-suffering figure

Pain and suffering is the most negotiated part of any claim. These factors move it most:

01

Severity & permanence

Temporary pain earns a low multiplier; permanent, life-altering injuries earn the highest. Permanence justifies the top of the 1.5–5 range.

02

Credibility & documentation

Pain diaries, consistent treatment and a believable account raise the figure; gaps and exaggeration sink it. Adjusters pay for what they can defend.

03

Method & venue

Which method an adjuster favours, and how generous local juries are, shifts the number. Running both methods shows the realistic band to negotiate within.

Before you run these tools, have your total medical bills (the multiplier base), your recovery timeline (the per-diem driver) and your attorney's contingency rate to hand. The closer those match reality, the more useful the result — though every figure here is illustrative and not a promise of what any insurer, employer or court will pay.
