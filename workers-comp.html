The method

How workers' comp settlements are calculated

Workers' compensation works nothing like an injury lawsuit. It is a no-fault system: you don't have to prove your employer did anything wrong, and your benefits aren't reduced because the accident was partly your own doing. The trade-off is that workers' comp pays no pain and suffering. There is no multiplier and no per-diem for emotional distress — those concepts simply don't apply here. Anyone telling you to plug medical bills into a "multiplier" for a comp claim is describing the wrong system.

Instead, a comp settlement is built from three concrete pieces. First, your average weekly wage (AWW) — usually based on your earnings in the 52 weeks before the injury — sets the rate for wage-replacement benefits, typically around two-thirds of your AWW up to a state maximum. Second, your impairment rating: when you reach maximum medical improvement (MMI), a doctor assigns a percentage reflecting permanent loss of function, and that percentage scales your permanent-disability award. Third, the value of your expected future medical care, which a lump-sum settlement may "buy out" so the insurer closes the file.

Every one of those pieces is governed by state law, not a national formula. States set their own weekly benefit rates, maximum caps, and "schedules" that assign a fixed number of weeks of benefits to specific body parts. That's why two workers with the identical injury and wage can settle for very different amounts depending on where they were hurt. The calculator uses these inputs — wage, rating and future care — rather than the fault-based math used everywhere else on SettleWorth.

What drives value

What determines a workers' comp lump sum

Because comp is formula-driven, the value comes from documentation, not argument. The pieces that matter most:

01

Impairment rating

Assigned at MMI, this percentage of permanent loss is the single biggest lever on a settlement. A disputed or low rating is often worth a second medical opinion.

02

Average weekly wage

An accurate AWW — including overtime, bonuses and second jobs where allowed — raises every wage-based benefit. An understated AWW quietly lowers the entire award.

03

Future medical costs

If your injury needs ongoing treatment or future surgery, closing out medical benefits in a lump sum should reflect that cost — once it's settled, the insurer no longer pays.

The biggest mistakes in comp claims are settling before MMI (you can't know your impairment until you've healed as much as you will), accepting the insurer's impairment rating without question, and overlooking future medical needs when agreeing to a full-and-final lump sum. Workers also lose value by reporting the injury late or missing scheduled appointments, both of which give the carrier reasons to dispute the claim. A separate issue: if a non-employer (a contractor, a product maker, a careless driver) caused your work injury, you may have a third-party case in addition to comp — and that one can include pain and suffering.

To estimate a lump sum here, you'll need your average weekly wage, your impairment rating once you've reached MMI, and a sense of your future treatment. All figures are illustrative and based on general methods — your actual award depends on your state's schedule and the facts of your claim.
